# The layout grid

Source: Josef Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design / Raster Systeme für die visuelle Gestaltung (Niggli, 1981, 176 pages). Section pages are from the book's own contents page, checked against the archive.org text; pages for individual sentences are the section's opening page. Tufte's chartjunk rule is about dark, dominant gridlines inside a chart (muted ones are allowed); the page grid here is a different thing, the discipline his own books are set in (one text column, one wide margin column for notes), so the two agree.

## Why a grid

Müller-Brockmann's claim (Grid and design philosophy, p. 10): working with the grid system means submitting to laws of universal validity; the aim is the greatest possible order and economy. The grid is an aid in organising text and illustration (What is the purpose of the grid?, p. 13), justified by economic reasons, rational reasons and a mental attitude. His own caveat, at the close of the 8-field section (p. 72): the grid is in itself no guarantee of success, a serviceable instrument and nothing more. Reading of the 32-field grid (p. 87): more fields give an almost unlimited number of solutions and grade picture sizes with more subtlety, and they need a high degree of self-discipline so that clarity and order do not degenerate into confusion.

## The elements, in the order the book decides them

1. **Format and margins** (Sizes of paper p. 15; Margin proportions p. 39). Fix the page or screen size, then the margins; the area inside is the type area where everything lives. A well-proportioned margin enhances the pleasure of reading; the great books calculated theirs with the Golden Section or another formula; margins too narrow make every trimming error leap to the eye. Aim for tension in the proportions rather than equal margins. (The classical canon of inner margin smallest and bottom largest is the usual answer in print; on screen, equal side margins and a larger top margin read well.)
2. **Type and leading** (Typeface alphabets p. 19; Leading p. 34; Body and display faces p. 45). Choose the body size for the reader and the reading distance (30 to 35 cm for print), then its leading: proper leading is one of the most important factors; too tight slows reading, too open leaves the reader uncertain. The leading is the vertical unit of the whole layout, the baseline grid. The book itself is set 9 pt on 12 pt with captions 7 pt on 8 pt, chosen so that caption lines and text lines share depths and realign.
3. **Columns** (Width of column p. 30). The rule of thumb is seven words per line; aim for seven to ten; an average of ten words per line is easy to read. The column count follows from that measure at the chosen size. Dashboards: twelve columns divide by two, three, four and six; single charts: six.
4. **Fields** (The typographic grid p. 11; Construction of the grid p. 57). Fields are separated by an intermediate space so pictures do not touch and captions can be placed below them. The vertical distance between fields is one, two or more lines of text; in the worked example one line is chosen, and because 57 lines minus three gaps does not divide by four, the column depth is changed to 55 lines, giving four equal fields of 13 lines. The horizontal gutter depends on type and illustration size; keep it constant. Müller-Brockmann's worked systems: 8 fields (p. 72, leaflets and brochures, subdividable to 16), 20 fields (p. 76, shown with 42 solutions), 32 fields (p. 87, the grid of the book itself). Take the fewest fields that place every element.
5. **Placement** (Construction of the type area p. 49; The photograph in the grid system p. 97). Every panel, text block, caption, table and image occupies whole fields, merged as needed; picture edges align with the ascenders and descenders of the text lines beside them. The fewer the differences in the size of the illustrations, the quieter the impression (p. 13). Nothing straddles a gutter; nothing starts between baselines.

## Rules for charts and dashboards

Müller-Brockmann wrote for print pages of text and pictures; the rules below are his method applied to panels, captions and tables, and are this skill's, not quotations.

- **Shared edges.** Panels in a row start and end on the same column lines; their y-axis labels either all hang into the gutter or none do. A row of panels and the table below it align on the same column lines.
- **A chart field includes its words.** The title above and the tick labels below belong to the chart's field: reserve a baseline for each inside the field, so the axes box sits one baseline in and the field's outer edges are what align.
- **Vertical rhythm.** Chart heights, the gap to a caption, the gap between sections and table row heights are all multiples of the baseline. A section gap is a whole field, not a line.
- **Captions and notes belong to their field.** The book places captions below pictures, in a smaller or italic face, with line depths that realign with the text; this skill sets a caption on the first baseline below its panel's field, and a note beside a panel with its first baseline on the panel field's first baseline, so note and panel top-align. A note beside a table aligns its first baseline with the table's header baseline. A caption takes the measure of the column it sits in.
- **One measure per span.** Running notes at seven to ten words per line; if a caption spans two panels, shorten it or set it in two columns, do not let it run to twenty words.
- **Tufte inside the field.** The chart's aspect ratio is still chosen by the data (wider than tall as the default); the field system decides where the chart sits and which whole fields it merges, not the slopes inside it.
- **One grid per document.** Sections may use different spans of the same columns; they never use different columns, gutters or baselines. On screens columns collapse in whole numbers (12 to 6 to 4) and the baseline never changes.
- **Type.** One family, body and caption sizes whose line depths realign, one weight for emphasis; flush left; no centered blocks. This is Tufte's friendly graphic and Müller-Brockmann's typography meeting.
- **Hung labels.** Axis tick labels and row labels may hang into the gutter to the left of the column line, consistently across every panel, so the data start on the column line.

## A dashboard template

Twelve columns, gutter two baselines (hung axis labels need the room), body 14 px on a 24 px baseline (print: 10 pt on 14 pt). A row of three panels with a note beside them: each panel spans three columns and twelve baselines (title and tick labels inside), the note spans the last three columns with its first baseline on the row's first baseline. A row of three panels without a note: four columns each. The row's caption spans the panel columns on the baseline after the row. A ledger table below spans eight columns, each row one baseline (two for rows with sparklines); its side note spans the last four columns, first baseline on the table header. Section gap: one field (six baselines). The same twelve columns carry the headline and the source line.

## Checking a layout (rubric D7)

Draw the grid over the render (`page.show()` in `tufte_mpl`, or a translucent overlay in a design tool): dotted column lines, dotted row edges, solid text baselines. Panel and table edges should sit on column lines and row edges; page typography (headline, panel titles, captions, notes, table rows) should sit on baselines; every gap should be a count of rows. Labels positioned by the data (tick labels, direct labels, notes and callouts at marks) follow the data, not the grid, and are exempt. An edge on no row edge, or page type on no baseline, is a finding.
