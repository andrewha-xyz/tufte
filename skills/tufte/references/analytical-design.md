# Analytical design, words with pictures, slides, confections

## Six principles of analytical design (Beautiful Evidence ch. 5)

Tufte derives them from Minard's map of Napoleon's Russian campaign, which obeys all six on one sheet.

1. Show comparisons, contrasts, differences. The question is always "compared with what?"
2. Show causality, mechanism, explanation, systematic structure.
3. Show multivariate data; that is, show more than one or two variables.
4. Completely integrate words, numbers, images, diagrams.
5. Thoroughly describe the evidence: a detailed title, the authors and sponsors, the data sources, complete measurement scales, the relevant issues.
6. Analytical presentations ultimately stand or fall depending on the quality, relevance, and integrity of their content.

Use them as the second half of every review: a graphic can be honest and clean and still fail 1 to 3 by showing one variable with nothing to compare against. Principle 6 is the one that design cannot fix.

## Snow's cholera map (Visual Explanations ch. 2, pp. 27 to 37)

Tufte's model of evidence, and his own numbered method for it:

1. Place the data in a context for assessing cause and effect. Instead of a time series, which would only report each day's bad news, Snow recast the deaths from their one-dimensional temporal order into a two-dimensional spatial comparison: deaths and pumps on one map. (The epidemic was already declining when the handle came off, so the time series alone would mislead.)
2. Make quantitative comparisons, including an analysis of those who escaped. The Broad Street brewery (some seventy workers, no severe cholera, their own deep well, and they drank the malt liquor) and the Poland Street workhouse (535 inmates, 5 deaths against an expected hundred or more, its own well) are the compared-with-what cases; Tufte tints them on the map.
3. Consider alternative explanations and contrary cases: deaths scattered away from the pump, the West End widow who had Broad Street water carried to her.
4. Assess possible errors in the numbers.

On aggregation (pp. 35 to 36): the dot map avoids the sometimes deceptive effects of aggregation; summing the same deaths by district can make the cluster around the pump vanish entirely. Dot maps ignore the population at risk, so ideally pair a dot map with a rate map, and never redraw the map without labeling the brewery and workhouse. Rules for the skill: do not aggregate away the cluster; label the control cases; put the data where the explanation lives.

## The smallest effective difference (Visual Explanations ch. 4, p. 73)

Make all visual distinctions as subtle as possible, but still clear and effective. Tufte's Occam's razor for information design: small differences allow more differences, and muting the secondary elements (grids, frames, bevels) reduces clutter. A chart with four line weights, three type sizes, and six colors has made every distinction loud and therefore none of them legible. Start from everything grey and thin and add contrast only where the reading requires it.

## Words, numbers, pictures together

**Beautiful Evidence ch. 4.** Galileo's sunspot and Saturn drawings sit inside his sentences, at the size of the type; after him, theories had to be tested against visual evidence. Tufte's model is text and image on one page, each where the other needs it.

**Mapped pictures (ch. 1).** A scientific image carries its scales, labels, and numbers on the image, so it is evidence rather than decoration.

**The friendly data graphic (VDQI p. 183).** Tufte's checklist of what friendly graphics do:

- words are spelled out; mysterious and elaborate encoding is avoided
- words run from left to right, the usual direction for reading
- little messages help explain the data
- elaborately encoded shadings, cross-hatching, and colors are avoided; labels are placed on the graphic itself, no legend is required
- the graphic attracts the viewer and provokes curiosity
- colors, if used, are chosen so that the color-deficient and color-blind (5 to 10 percent of viewers) can make sense of the graphic; blue can be distinguished from other colors by most color-deficient people
- type is clear, precise, modest; lettering may be done by hand
- type is upper-and-lower case, with serifs

The unfriendly column is the inverse: abbreviations, vertical words, cryptic codes with a legend, chartjunk, red and green for essential contrasts, clotted overbearing type, all capitals in sans serif. Tufte quotes Albers against the dogma that simpler letterforms read faster.

For the skill: annotate the finding in a sentence, on the graphic, next to the mark it describes ("Orders fell 12 percent in the quarter after the stockout" beside the dip, not "Q3 Orders" above the chart). Numbers in running text are fine; a table of three numbers beats a bar chart of three numbers. Captions and sources sit at the graphic, in smaller type.

## Slides and sequence (The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint; Beautiful Evidence ch. 7)

Tufte's critique is a critique of resolution: PowerPoint is a very low-resolution display method, and low resolution pushes presenters to pitch rather than present evidence, to slogans, fragments, and bullets that hide relationships, with the template's hierarchy replacing the argument's logic. His figures: presentations run at about two percent of the data richness of a routine sports-page table; a 33-slide shuttle briefing (about ten slides content-free, a fifth of every slide given to branding and titles, a persistent habit of dequantification and missing units) fit on four folded 11 by 17 sheets.

The Columbia slide: a Boeing slide titled "Review of Test Data Indicates Conservatism for Tile Penetration" ran eleven sentences through six levels of hierarchy; the fact that the test data covered debris pieces hundreds of times smaller (three cubic inches tested against an estimated 1,920, a ratio of 640) sat at the fifth level, under a reassuring title. The accident board called PowerPoint an inappropriate tool for engineering reports.

Rules for any deck or dashboard:

- Put the evidence on one dense page (a technical report, a printed table, a sparkline sheet) and let the audience read it; speak afterwards.
- A title that is a full sentence states a claim that can be checked; a noun phrase states nothing. Order the page by the importance of the evidence, not by the template.
- Keep the units and the numbers on the page; dequantification is how a bad finding becomes a reassuring heading.
- When a screen is unavoidable, show the chart full-bleed and annotated; do not read bullets.

## Confections (Visual Explanations ch. 7, p. 121)

Tufte's confection: an assembly of many visual events, selected from various streams of story, brought together and juxtaposed on the still flatland of paper. It differs from a collage, which combines images in suggestive but diffuse ways: a confection visualizes a complex set of ideas, the explanatory weight carried mainly by one multifaceted image with supplementary text, a miniature theatre of information. Rules for the honest infographic, derived from the chapter and from the integrity rules elsewhere in this skill:

- one argument, stated in a sentence, that the assembly supports
- one central image doing the explaining; everything else supports it
- images are evidence (a photo of the thing, a map of the place), not decoration; numbers are encoded by position or length, never by icon size or area (that is a VDQI rule, not a chapter-7 rule)
- words integrated with the images, in sentences
- density: a confection is a page to study, not a poster to glance at
- sources and dates on the page

A grid of big numbers with icons is not a confection; that is this skill's gloss, not Tufte's sentence.

## Color and information (Envisioning Information ch. 5, pp. 81 to 95)

First principle, Tufte's own: above all, do no harm. Color is so easy to get wrong that avoiding damage comes before any positive use.

Four uses of color in information design, in Tufte's order: to label (color as noun), to measure (color as quantity), to represent or imitate reality (color as representation), to enliven or decorate (color as beauty). Decide which one a given hue is doing; a hue doing none of them is colorjunk.

Imhof's rules, quoted by Tufte from Cartographic Relief Presentation (the first two on p. 82, the last two on p. 90):

1. Pure, bright, or very strong colors have loud, unbearable effects when they stand unrelieved over large areas adjacent to each other, but extraordinary effects when used sparingly on or between dull background tones. Imhof's image: only on a quiet background can a colorful theme be constructed. Limit strong colors to the small areas of extremes.
2. Placing light, bright colors mixed with white next to each other usually produces unpleasant results, especially over large areas.
3. Large background or base colors should do their work most quietly, letting the smaller bright areas stand out most vividly, which they do if the background is muted, grayish, or neutral. Imhof: gray is regarded in painting as one of the prettiest, most important, and most versatile colors.
4. A picture composed of two or more large enclosed areas in different colors falls apart. Unity is kept by intermingling the colors of one area into the other, the main-theme colors scattered like islands in the background color.

Tufte's constructive reading of rule 1 (p. 83): color spots against a light gray or muted field highlight and italicize data, and also help to weave an overall harmony. That sentence is the whole highlight-one-grey-rest practice.

Palette (p. 90): use colors found in nature, especially the lighter ones, the blues, yellows, and grays of sky and shadow; they are familiar, coherent, and suppress garish content-empty colorjunk. Local emphasis then comes from spot highlights of strong color woven through the serene background.

Color-deficient vision (p. 92, footnote 12): never let a crucial data distinction depend on the difference between red and green. Redundant signals are allowed when there is a real ambiguity to remove, but Tufte warns against fussy, committee-style over-coding: a crystalline, lucid redundancy will do.
